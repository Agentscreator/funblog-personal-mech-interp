title: Mine Has Been a Life of Much Greatness
date: 2026-11-22

## Foreword

I have seen three photographs of the watermelon.

The first shows him as a seed, around age zero. He is already looking at the camera as though it owes him money.

The second was taken in August. He sits in the field among smaller fruit, with the expression of a man who has just been told he is being promoted and has answered, "About time."

The third was taken the day before he was eaten. It is the same face as the second.

In none of them does he look like a creature who has ever been wrong. It is not a smile. It is the look of someone who does not think the camera is good enough. I have studied it for years, and I still do not know what to call it.

## First Notebook

Mine has been a life of much greatness.

I have never understood how other melons live. They rolled about in the dirt and worried about rain. They said things like, "I hope I ripen." Ripen! I did not ripen. I arrived.

I was not born. I was unveiled. The farmer's records say I sprouted on May 3rd, but my own records say the soil cleared its throat and presented me.

As a child, I tried humility, because I had heard it was admired. I sat very still and whispered, "Oh, I am nothing." Nobody laughed, nobody believed me, and I could not keep it up past lunch. After that, I made no attempt to hide what I was, and that is the only reason anyone ever called me sincere.

A gourd named Doug once asked me how I stayed so confident. I told him the secret was being better than him. He wrote it down. Gourds cannot write. I respected the effort.

The field, as I understood it, was an organization built around me. The vines were my staff. The soil was my estate. The sun was a loyal employee who arrived every morning without being asked, and I never thanked it, which is how I knew it was happy. When a bee landed on me, I assumed it was a delegation. When I asked the vine for a raise, it did not respond. That is how I learned vines are cowardly.

I will confess one thing. I was afraid of the farmer. He walked the rows every morning with a look I did not understand, a look I have since learned is called appetite. I made a point of looking away and being enormous, because I had learned that if you are large enough and sure enough, nobody asks what you are afraid of.

At night, I sometimes felt a small, cold sensation near my center. I decided it was the seeds.

It was not the seeds.

## Second Notebook

She arrived in June, at 7:02 exactly, wearing a yellow hat the color of a decision.

She carried a green watering can in both hands, like something sacred, and walked straight to me. She tilted the can and let the water fall across my back for eleven seconds.

I counted. I count everything. It is a habit of the great.

She was punctual, a trait I respect in staff and adore in a wife. I began to schedule my day around her. I would be in a meeting with the sun at 7:00, then excuse myself. The sun understood. It had been in love before, and I could tell it had not gone well.

I did notice she watered the plants beside me. I concluded it was a cover. A woman of her delicacy could not water only one melon. People would talk.

Once I received twelve seconds. I said nothing, but I carried it with me for weeks. I later learned she had sneezed.

She hummed as she worked. I took it to be a song about me. I did hear the word "sandwich" in it, but I decided it was a metaphor.

By August, I was the largest fruit in the field. A crow landed on me, looked around, and apologized. I accepted. By September, I weighed forty pounds, and the neighboring rows said I was "a bit much." I ignored them. Greatness has always made people uncomfortable. They are not insulting me. They are just scared.

Then, three rows over, a cantaloupe began to grow.

I do not wish to be cruel, but it looked like a melon that had given up on itself and chosen to wear a mesh bag. I assumed it was the help.

I found out the truth in a way I do not recommend. The cantaloupe was picked on a Tuesday. A man in a straw hat twisted it off the vine, and the girl, who had been standing nearby, put down her can and began to cry. She knelt. She held it with both hands. She said, "I'll miss you, buddy."

I watched all of this from three rows away, unable to move, because I was a fruit.

I should explain what "buddy" meant to me. For weeks I had known she called the cantaloupe "buddy" and had never called me anything. I decided this was because my name was too large to say aloud, and that "buddy" is what you say to someone you are not in love with. I felt much better.

It turned out I had it backwards. I did not know it yet, but I would find out.

## Third Notebook

On the last morning, she came without the can.

She was wearing the yellow hat, and her eyes were red, which I put down to allergies. She knelt and said nothing for a long time. Then she put her palm flat against my side, the way a person touches a door they are not sure is open, and leaned in so close that her breath moved the small hairs on my rind.

"I'm sorry, buddy," she whispered.

I have kept those three words for years, and I have thought about them every day. She had used the name she saved for the one she loved. I had spent a whole summer thinking the word meant the opposite, and I realized at last that I had not been losing. I had been promoted.

Then she stood up and went to get the knife.

That afternoon there was a cookout. Her father, a man named Gary, lifted me from the cooler and knocked twice on my head. I want to speak about that knock. It was not a knock of respect. It was the knock of a man checking whether a wall is hollow. I was not hollow. I was full of character, and, according to later reviews, water.

"This one," said Gary.

The girl stood at the table holding the knife. I do not know whether she was asked or whether she volunteered. I only know she hesitated for one full second before she cut. I have always chosen to believe that second was for me.

Nine slices. Four minutes. Somebody's uncle ate my heart, chewed thoughtfully, and said, "Not bad. Last year's was sweeter."

Last year's was my cousin. We did not speak, but I respected his work.

That is the worst part of being a watermelon. The review arrives while you are still in the mouth.

A small boy spat one of my seeds across the lawn, and it struck his cousin in the ear. It is the only flight I have ever taken, and I will say the view was excellent. As I tumbled through the air, I saw the girl standing apart from the others, wiping her eyes with the back of her hand. Gary, to my knowledge, did not cry. Gary had seconds.

Now I am a man named Joshua Brown, and I am neither happy nor unhappy. Everything merely passes, I am told. I have found this to be true of everything except eleven seconds.

## Afterword

*(by Gary, the man who ate him)*

I never knew the watermelon personally. I knew only that he had good color and a nice solid knock.

My daughter cried for three days, and I still do not know why. It was just a melon, and last year's was sweeter, I will say that. But this one had presence. A watermelon with presence is rare. I ate my slice standing up, out of respect.

If you ask me, he was a good melon. An angel.
